Who speaks up?

Seniority language and aviation safety as a social graph. When a junior pilot sees the danger first, how do they say it, and does the cockpit hierarchy decide whether anyone listens? This page lays out the project against every week of the course: the theory, the data, the model, and the checks.

Week 8 asks for this, on one slide

The project on one slide

The course's project brief: a question in one sentence, a dataset with both networks and text, a measurement from both halves of the course, a check, and the one figure you can already picture. This is that slide.

Question
When a junior pilot sees the danger first, is the warning phrased as a hint instead of a command, and has that seniority language become more direct since crew resource management (CRM) training began in 1981?
Dataset
Macro graph: airliner-accident articles on Wikipedia (nodes) linked by the links in their prose (edges), with the article text on each node; a few hundred accidents, crawled with the week-1 pipeline.
Micro graphs: for each accident with a published cockpit-voice-recorder (CVR) transcript, one small directed graph per flight: crew roles as nodes, every utterance as an edge carrying its text.
Measurement
A mitigation level (1 = command … 6 = hint) for every crew utterance, and from it the authority gradient of each flight: how much more indirect speech travels up the hierarchy than down. Network side: PageRank, communities and assortativity of the accident graph. Language side: TF-IDF, Scattertext, embeddings and an LLM-labelled, hand-validated mitigation instrument.
Check
Shuffle speaker labels inside each transcript (is the gradient bigger than chance?); shuffle years (is the CRM change real?); hand-label 300 utterances and report Cohen's κ against the instrument. We abandon the question if the gradient is indistinguishable from the speaker-shuffle null, or κ < 0.6.
The figure
Authority gradient per flight against year, with the 1981 CRM line and the shuffled-speaker null as a grey band. One dot per accident, sized by its PageRank in the macro graph, so the "teaching cases" stand out.

Two graphs and a bridge

The model

Week 8 names two ways to join networks and language: text as an attribute on nodes (tested with a shuffle), and text as a network (entities or speakers connected by what they say, tested by reading a sample of edges). This project uses both, on two scales.

1 · The micro graph: one per flight

For flight f, the CVR transcript becomes a directed multigraph. Nodes are the people on the recording (captain CPT, first officer FO, flight engineer FE, air traffic control ATC, cabin crew CA). Every utterance is an edge from speaker to addressee, carrying its text and its mitigation level m.

G_f = (V_f, E_f), V_f ⊆ {CPT, FO, FE, ATC, CA} e = (speaker, addressee, text, m), m ∈ {1 … 6} authority gradient AG_f = mean m(FO→CPT) − mean m(CPT→FO) warning uptake U_f = share of hazard call-outs that change what the addressee does next

2 · The macro graph: the accident record

Nodes are accidents, edges are prose links between their articles, weighted by link count. Each node carries attributes: year, CRM era, the crew's seniority gap (captain vs first-officer hours, from the final reports), the airline country's power-distance index, and from the micro graph, its authority gradient AG and uptake U.

3 · The bridge

The micro graph's numbers become node attributes of the macro graph. Then the network questions become testable: are high-gradient accidents the teaching cases (PageRank)? Do they cluster (assortativity on AG against a shuffle)? Did the gradient fall after 1981?

The mitigation scale

Fischer & Orasanu's six levels, from most to least direct, with their weather-deviation examples. "Mitigated speech" is anything that softens a request out of deference.

1Command"Turn thirty degrees right." 2Crew obligation"I think we need to deviate right about now." 3Crew suggestion"Let's go around the weather." 4Query"Which direction would you like to deviate?" 5Preference"I think it would be wise to turn left or right." 6Hint"That return at twenty-five miles looks mean."

A sketch of one micro graph: arrows are utterances, darker means more indirect. In a steep cockpit the arrows going up to the captain are the dark ones.

Hypotheses, each with its null

H1 · The gradient exists.

AG > 0: speech up the hierarchy is more mitigated than speech down it.
Null: permute speaker roles within each transcript, 1,000 times.

H2 · CRM flattened it.

AG is lower after 1981, and lower where power distance is lower.
Null: shuffle years across flights; control for airline country.

H3 · The record remembers it.

High-AG accidents have higher PageRank: they became the cases that later reports cite.
Null: degree-preserving rewiring of the macro graph; shuffled AG.

The authority gradient

A toy model of one hazard. The first officer spots it and warns the captain. How directly the warning is phrased depends on how steep the cockpit is; how likely the captain is to act depends on how direct it was. With CRM, an ignored warning is repeated more directly; without it, it is repeated the same way. Move the sliders and watch how many of 2,000 simulated hazards get caught.

The numbers inside the model (how likely a captain acts on each level) are illustrative assumptions, not measurements. Estimating them from the transcripts is the project.

Checks, and the roads not taken

Every number, and what checks it

  • Mitigation level (LLM or fine-tuned classifier, 🔬): 300 utterances hand-labelled by two of us; report κ between us and between us and the model; publish the confusion matrix.
  • Authority gradient: speaker-role permutation null within each transcript.
  • CRM effect: year shuffle; the same test on ASRS near-miss narratives as a second dataset.
  • Communities: modularity against degree-preserving nulls (shown above), Louvain vs Infomap NMI, and NMI against Wikipedia's own cause categories.
  • Teaching cases: PageRank vs in-degree, so a "central" accident is not just a well-linked one.
  • Name resolution: a hand-read sample of 50 report↔article matches.

Two ideas we killed (and keep)

ATC phraseology across countries. Pilot–controller radio as a network, with native vs non-native English speakers. Killed: transcripts are scarce and mostly in accident reports anyway, so it is a subset of this project.

The citation graph of safety recommendations. NTSB recommendations citing earlier ones. Killed: great network, very little text per node. Kept as insurance in case the CVR set is too small.

The course's advice: around week 12 a third of groups find a hole in their data. A written-down alternative means switching takes a day.
